 The baseline scenario projected a global temperature increase of 3.3°C by 2100. I introduced several policy actions. Discouraging coal use reduced projected warming to 3.1°C, while discouraging oil use reduced it further. Finally, lowering the assumptions for population and economic growth brought the projected warming in my final scenario to 2.6°C by 2100.
The scenario therefore reduced projected warming by approximately 0.7°C compared with the 3.3°C baseline. This is a meaningful improvement, but it does not achieve the objective of limiting warming to well below 2°C. Consequently, the climate impacts may be less severe than in the baseline scenario, but they cannot yet be considered fully manageable.
Compared with the 2000s, the impacts shown in my selected graphs (i.e., 'global sources of primary energy' and 'greenhouse gas net emissions') were more severe in 2050 and especially in 2100. The mitigation measures reduced the severity of these future impacts compared with the baseline, but they did not eliminate them.
Adaptation policies would still be necessary. Mitigation reduces the scale of future climate change, while adaptation helps communities, ecosystems, and infrastructure cope with impacts that are already occurring or cannot be avoided. Examples include heat-response plans, flood protection, water management, climate-resilient infrastructure, and adaptation in agriculture.

My answer: 
- To support a healthy economy, I examined the Marginal Cost of Electricity Production graph while developing my scenario. My policy package included a reduction in coal use, a smaller reduction in oil and nuclear energy, and increased use of renewables and new zero-carbon energy. I also introduced a higher carbon price.

- In the transport sector, I made a small improvement in energy efficiency and a much larger shift towards electrification. I applied similar changes to buildings and industry. I also reduced projected population and economic growth.
Further adjustments included increasing both nature-based and technological carbon dioxide removal, reducing agricultural emissions, limiting waste and leakage, and reducing deforestation. Together, these policies lowered projected warming to 2.1°C by 2100. This is a considerable improvement from the 3.3°C baseline, but it remains slightly above the required target of less than 2°C or the 1.5°C.

- Growth and equity considerations: I explored the advanced settings for population and economic growth by selecting the three dots beside each slider. I set population growth below the UN projection range. For economic growth, I adjusted the short-term and long-term growth assumptions and set the transition period to 100 years.
These additional changes did not visibly change the rounded temperature projection, which remained at 2.1°C. This suggests that, within the settings I tested, these adjustments had less influence on the temperature outcome than changes to energy, emissions, and land use.
However, changing population and economic growth does not by itself address equity. Equity would require considering how the costs and benefits of the transition are distributed. For example, carbon-pricing revenue could support lower-income households, clean-energy access, worker retraining, and communities that currently depend on fossil-fuel industries.

- Impacts on air, oceans, and biodiversity: The Air Pollution from Energy by Source—PM2.5 graph shows that pollution from coal, oil, gas, and bioenergy is lower in my scenario than in the baseline. This represents an important public-health benefit, particularly for communities exposed to high levels of air pollution.
In the loss in ocean life from warming graph, impacts on marine animals, zooplankton, and phytoplankton become more noticeable after approximately 2040 and continue increasing towards 2100. Therefore, my scenario reduces some climate pressures but does not prevent further harm to marine ecosystems, given the observed increase in the loss after 2040 onwards.
The species losing more than 50% of climatic Range graph shows that invertebrates, insects, and plants face the greatest risks. Approximately 21–24% of species in these categories are projected to lose more than half of their climatic range, compared with approximately 10% of mammals. These findings demonstrate that substantial biodiversity risks remain under a 2.1°C scenario settings.

  - Surprises
      I was surprised that combining many policies still produced projected warming of 2.1°C. Changes to population and economic growth had a smaller visible effect than I expected, while reducing fossil-fuel use, improving efficiency, electrifying end uses, and controlling non-CO2 emissions had more noticeable effects. I was also surprised that environmental damage continued even after achieving a substantial reduction from the baseline. This demonstrates the delays within the climate system and the importance of acting early for sustainable future.

  - Hope
      The expansion of renewable energy, improvements in energy efficiency, and increasing electrification give me hope that this scenario is possible. En-ROADS shows that no single measure is sufficient, but coordinated action across energy, transport, buildings, industry, agriculture, waste, and land use can considerably reduce warming. The air-quality benefits also show that climate policies can produce improvements in human health before the full climate benefits are realized.
